# Comedy Science & Writing Handbook

This is the project's craft reference. It teaches mechanisms, not imitation.

## 1. The comic premise

A premise is the central observation that makes the situation funny. A strong premise contains tension, contradiction, recognition or an unusual point of view.

Weak: “This celebrity is rich.”

Stronger premise shape: “The more money someone has, the more ordinary problems they can turn into luxury problems.”

The example is a structural demonstration, not a finished joke.

## 2. Core mechanisms

- **Incongruity:** Put two incompatible frames together.
- **Misdirection:** Lead toward a plausible expectation, then reveal a different interpretation.
- **Reversal:** Flip the assumed power dynamic or meaning.
- **Exaggeration:** Extend a recognizable truth beyond realistic limits.
- **Understatement:** Describe an outrageous event with calm, disproportionate restraint.
- **Specificity:** Replace generic labels with concrete details.
- **Rule of three:** Establish two beats, then break the pattern with the third.
- **Tags:** Add short follow-up punches after a strong laugh line.
- **Callback:** Reintroduce an earlier detail with a changed meaning.
- **Act-out:** Make the audience hear or see a person, thought or situation.
- **Escalation:** Increase stakes or absurdity in a coherent progression.
- **Satire:** Expose contradictions, incentives, hypocrisy or social absurdity.

## 3. Why jokes can fail

- The audience can predict the punchline.
- The setup is longer than the payoff deserves.
- The comparison is familiar or random rather than revealing.
- The joke explains itself after the laugh line.
- The writer confuses insult with observation.
- The reference is too niche for the intended audience.
- The story is old, unclear or factually uncertain.
- The joke attacks an irrelevant personal trait instead of the public behaviour.

## 4. Short-form architecture

For a 40–45 second spoken script, target roughly 82–108 words, then test aloud. Delivery speed varies.

A useful shape:
1. 0–3 seconds: a sharp hook or surprising opinion.
2. 3–10 seconds: orient the viewer with the essential fact.
3. 10–28 seconds: reveal the comic premise and escalate.
4. 28–40 seconds: strongest comparison, reversal or act-out.
5. Final beat: memorable payoff; no generic “what do you think?” unless it adds value.

This is a flexible map, not a formula every script must visibly follow.

## 5. Comedy writers' room

Generate different angles before drafting:
- Observational
- Savage roast of public conduct
- Absurdist analogy
- Storytelling / act-out
- Contradiction / hypocrisy

Then choose one dominant premise. Do not cram every style into one 45-second script.

## 6. Originality

Study the technique of comedians and writers, never their protected expression. Do not reproduce recognizable bits, punchlines, signature catchphrases or a living comedian's distinctive voice. Build a consistent original narrator.

## 7. The audience test

A model score is not proof of laughter. Human review and performance analytics are the evidence:
- first-three-second retention
- average view duration / percentage viewed
- replays
- shares
- comments that quote or extend a joke
- qualitative feedback from viewers

Keep a record of approved and rejected scripts and why.
